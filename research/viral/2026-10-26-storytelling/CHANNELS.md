# Stiff pictures, gripping shorts: what limited-animation channels do (2026-10-26)

Question: our 2 shorts felt stiff; Tập 1 averaged 45.5 % viewed (viewers gone at ~0:18 of 40 s). Which hugely watched
channels have crude, limited or almost static animation, and how do they hold attention anyway?

**Evidence used, strongest first**
- **[M] Our own measurements** of the reference shorts: `TRANSCRIPTS.md` (words/s, opening lines, endings, from captions)
  and `../2026-10-26-motion/NOTES.md` (cuts, motion, still seconds). View counts from `../2026-10-23-channels/candidates.md`.
- **[P] Primary or near-primary sources**: creator quotes in Wikipedia/press, an academic article on storytime animation,
  Chuck Jones's "illustrated radio" test.
- **[S] Secondary descriptions** (reviews, wikis). Used only to describe a style, not to explain why it works.

**Honest gap:** the web search turned up almost no first-person "how I write" interviews from TomSka, Sam O'Nella,
Casually Explained, Brewstew or Haminations. Reddit and TV Tropes were blocked for fetching, and the Cartoon Brew YouTube
animator panel was blocked too. So the "why it works" column below is mostly **our inference from measured videos**, with
confidence marked: **H** = measured or creator-stated, **M** = consistent across several channels but inferred,
**L** = single source or our guess.

Not useful for us: Alan Becker / Animator vs Animation and Dick Figures action (they win on motion we can't make);

**The one-line pattern:** the static channels win with **density of voice**, not motion. Casually Explained is the
proof: 86 % still seconds, 3.9M views, because a joke lands every sentence. We have the stillness of Casually Explained
but talk at 1.2–1.3 words/s (a third of its rate), so the viewer gets neither motion nor words.

## 2. Techniques for UNIT 4B (stiff animation, international English audience)

**T1. Voice carries the video: 2.5–3.5 words per second from second 0.** — Conf. **H**
Evidence: references talk 2.2–3.7 w/s, we talk 1.2–1.3 (TRANSCRIPTS.md); "illustrated radio" (Jones/Hanna-Barbera);
Ricky Gervais Show is literally a podcast with pictures. Apply: a 30 s short = 75–100 spoken words. Sgt. Snuggles'
documentary/noir narration is our engine; let him talk continuously and let Steve's lines cut in. Read the script aloud
with the picture off: if it isn't funny as audio, the drawings won't save it.

**T2. Premise in the first sentence, as a judgement, by ~2 s.** — Conf. **H**
Evidence: "My toxic trait is…", "One time…", "I need to… but…", "What people get wrong is…" (TRANSCRIPTS.md). Tập 1's
first frame gave the ending away and had no line. Apply: Snuggles opens with a claim, not a scene-setter:
"The adult human believes a delivery window is a promise." Never open on a title card or silence.

**T3. A laugh every 5–8 seconds; one joke per video is not enough.** — Conf. **H**
Evidence: Casually Explained lists put a joke in every sentence; Haminations adds asides between beats. Our Tập 1 died at
0:14–0:21, a 5–6 s stretch with nothing new (PERFORMANCE.md). Apply: mark every 6 s of the script; each mark needs a
new laugh or a new piece of information. If a beat only repeats ("he waits longer"), cut it.

**T4. List / escalation structure ("every type of…", "stage 1, 2, 3").** — Conf. **H**
Evidence: Casually Explained's top shorts are lists (3.9M, 2.6M); Llamas with Hats escalates per episode; asdfmovie is
a list of sketches. Lists suit static art because each item is a new still. Apply: "The five stages of waiting for a
parcel, narrated by Sgt. Snuggles"; "Every type of houseguest, ranked by Nana". One drawing per item, item 5 breaks
the pattern.

**T5. Literal visualisation: the picture shows exactly what the words say, or the absurd literal version.** — Conf. **H**
Evidence: Ricky Gervais Show animates Karl's anecdotes literally, and critics found the pictures funnier than the audio;
storytime channels cut to "what I pictured" (channels NOTES.md). Apply: when Snuggles says "the human enters a state of
hibernation", show Steve as a labelled specimen in a museum case. A new still per sentence is cheaper than animating
one long action, and it's funnier.

**T6. Cut, don't move: a new shot or pose on every phrase.** — Conf. **H**
Evidence: Haminations 0.3–0.9 s per shot, TheOdd1sOut ~1 s; our 2.5–3 s (motion NOTES.md). Casually Explained gets away
with 8 s shots only because words fill them. Apply: we can't animate fluidly, so change the drawing: hold-cut-hold.
Zoom to Steve's face, cut to the phone, cut to Snuggles. Rule: either new words or a new picture every ~1.5 s, ideally both.

**T7. The deadpan voice + the big reaction stays small, the exaggeration goes in the picture.** — Conf. **H** (internal) / **M**
Evidence: Casually Explained's monotone; Carl in Llamas with Hats calmly describing horrors; our earlier finding that
Steve over-reacting felt fake (channels NOTES.md). Apply: Snuggles narrates a disaster as wildlife footage, flat.
Steve sighs, mutters, refreshes the app. The cutaway (melting face, tiny drawn thought) carries the size.

**T8. Straight man + deadpan monster (two-hander).** — Conf. **M**
Evidence: Llamas with Hats (120M+ views, two near-static characters); Dick Figures; Gervais teasing Karl. A static
two-shot works when the dialogue is a ping-pong of normal vs insane. Apply: Steve (normal, anxious) vs Penny, the
8-year-old landlord, who calmly charges him a "breathing fee". Steve's reasonable objections are the setups;
Penny's calm answers are the punches.

**T9. Real, specific, everyday detail beats invented lore.** — Conf. **H**
Evidence: the biggest storytime shorts are tiny ordinary problems (pet a dog 55M, cracked tooth 39M, restaurant out of
meat 8.8M); Simon's Cat succeeds on "the funny little things they do" and keeps the cat unnamed so you see your own
cat. Matches our rule `unit4b-jokes-real-life-first`. Apply: "the 'sorry we missed you' note when you never left the
house", "typing 'ok' vs 'ok.'", the exact £ or $ price, the exact 2:47 PM. No joke that needs to know who Dead Ted is.

**T10. Steal the documentary/second-person frame for Snuggles.** — Conf. **M**
Evidence: Haminations' "My toxic trait" (12M) is built on nature-documentary logic and ends by turning the narrator into
the prey. Apply: Snuggles narrating Steve as a species is exactly that frame. End by flipping it: Snuggles fails at the
same thing ("…the hunter, too, is out of treats").

**T11. End with a reframe, a self-own, or a rule-of-two topper, then loop.** — Conf. **H**
Evidence: reference endings: reframe ("if this was a nature documentary you'd root for me"), self-own ("I'd say I
learned my lesson, but I still don't"), a second worse example (TRANSCRIPTS.md). Replays count as views since
31 Mar 2025 ([CoSchedule](https://coschedule.com/social-media-marketing/youtube-metrics)). Apply: last line flows into the
first line ("…which is why the human, once again, believes a delivery window is a promise.").

**T12. Short when it's one sight gag (≤ 18 s); longer only if jokes keep coming.** — Conf. **M**
Evidence: "Can I pet that dog?" 14 s and "I'm so in shape" 18 s (98M combined) are one setup + one visual twist;
comedy Shorts reportedly peak at 11–25 s ([Opus](https://www.opus.pro/blog/ideal-youtube-shorts-length-format-retention),
vendor data, low reliability). Apply: a single-idea episode (the missed-delivery note) should be ~15–20 s, not 40 s.

**T13. Sound effects and music as the "animation".** — Conf. **M**
Evidence: Hanna-Barbera carried action in sound; Pencilmation is wordless and relies on cartoon SFX; Simon's Cat is
animated first and sound-designed after. Apply: a still Steve + a phone buzz, a door slam off-screen, a sad trombone,
a noir sax sting under Snuggles. Sound tells time passing without drawing it.

**T14. Kinetic text: 1–3 words on screen, mid-frame, on the stressed word.** — Conf. **M**
Evidence: reference animators use big centred word captions (motion NOTES.md); Casually Explained uses labels and graphs
as punchlines; Sam O'Nella's corner icons as silent asides. Apply: label the specimen ("ADULT MALE. UNEMPLOYED BY
CHOICE?"), a graph of Steve's hope over the delivery window, a "1/5 STARS" stamp. Works for viewers watching muted.

**T15. A visual aside that comments without stopping the story.** — Conf. **L**
Evidence: Sam O'Nella's chess-engine "blunder" markers over a historical source (TV Tropes, user-edited). Apply: a tiny
Dead Ted in the corner silently rating Steve's choices; never explained, rewards rewatches.

## 3. What to avoid

1. **Silent stretches longer than one beat.** Static drawings + no words is the only combination none of the references
   use (except a 1-beat pause before a punch). Our 5–6 s silent montage is where Tập 1 lost people. **H**
2. **A hook that answers itself.** Don't show the punchline object (the note) in frame 1. Ask, don't tell. **H**
3. **Repeating one idea to show time passing.** Escalate (worse, weirder, more specific) or cut. Llamas with Hats'
   creator: Carl only kept surprising by doing something surprising. **H**
4. **Big acted emotion.** Screaming or sobbing reads fake on a normal-life problem; keep it flat, put size in the picture. **H**
5. **Jokes that need lore** (who Dead Ted is, how Penny became a landlord). Each short must work for a stranger. **H**
6. **Motion for its own sake.** Our v1 wobble/boil experiment caused jitter and glitches; every move needs a reason
   (motion NOTES v2). Cutting between good stills beats bad tweening. **H**
7. **One joke stretched to 40 s.** Either add laughs (every 5–8 s) or shorten to ~15–20 s. **H**
8. **Third-person narration with no attitude.** Snuggles must judge, not describe ("the human waits" → "the human
   waits, as is his tradition, in the least dignified position available"). **M**
9. **Cultural or wordplay jokes that don't travel.** International audience: visual and situational humour first,
   simple English, no puns that need a native ear. **M**
10. **Edgy-for-edgy's-sake darkness.** Cyanide/Llamas go dark, but on a mundane setup with a twist; our brand is
    everyday relatable. Keep dark jokes rare and earned. **L**

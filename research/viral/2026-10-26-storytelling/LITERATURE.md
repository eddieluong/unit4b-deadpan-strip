# Storytelling for near-static comedy shorts: literature review

Date: 2026-10-26. Written for UNIT 4B (stick figures that hold poses, 25-35 s Shorts, international English audience).
Problem: Tập 1 felt stiff. Average viewed was 45.5% of 40 s, with the swipe cliff at 0:18.

Confidence tags:
- **[E-high]**: peer-reviewed, replicated or large-sample evidence.
- **[E-med]**: one peer-reviewed study, a small sample, or a conference paper.
- **[E-low]**: preprint, industry or vendor data, or a secondhand report of a study.
- **[O]**: expert or practitioner opinion and craft tradition, not tested.

Some sources were read only through abstracts or secondary summaries. Those are marked "(via summary)".

## 1. Limited animation as a craft

### Key findings

1. **Hanna-Barbera moved the job from motion to sound and dialogue.**
   - In 1957 Hanna and Barbera split each character into a few cel levels and animated only the part that moved; the rest sat on a held cel.
   - Accounts say a 10-minute short needed about 1,200 drawings instead of about 26,000.
   - Scholars of the studio's sound argue that it "would seek animation's promise of life elsewhere, namely, through sound": stock music cues, signature sound effects and dialogue-led gags.
   - Chuck Jones called it "illustrated radio." His test was whether you could follow the story with your eyes closed.
   - It was a rival's insult, but it describes the model accurately: if the visuals barely move, the soundtrack has to carry the gag.
   - Sources [E-low/O]:
     - Wikipedia, "Limited animation": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_animation
     - MediaCommons / In Transition, "Sound in Hanna-Barbera" (video essay and creator's statement): https://mediacommons.org/intransition/node/792 and https://intransition.openlibhums.org/article/id/11337
     - Laughing Squid summary: https://laughingsquid.com/sound-in-hanna-barbera-animation/
2. **UPA treated reduction as a design choice, not just a budget cut.**
   - *Gerald McBoing-Boing* (1950, Oscar winner) cut the image down to "a few elemental lines", with no walls, floors or horizon lines, and used flat colour that shifts with the mood.
   - In some scenes the setting changes around a Gerald who stays still, instead of cutting to a new shot.
   - The lesson for us: a static character with a changing background or colour field still reads as motion and story.
   - Sources [O, historical]:
     - TCM, "UPA cartoons": https://www.tcm.com/articles/499721/upa-cartoons
     - Animation Obsessive, "An Introduction to UPA": https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/an-introduction-to-upa
     - Wikipedia, "Gerald McBoing-Boing": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_McBoing-Boing
3. **Tezuka and anime: hold most of the time and spend motion on the key moment.**
   - Astro Boy (1963) used fewer drawings per second, reused cels (a "bank" of stock shots), and put body parts on separate layers so only the moving part was redrawn.
   - It also used manga panels as storyboards.
   - Anime production saves on low-motion shots so the budget can go to one demanding cut. Action is animated on 2s and 3s.
   - Osamu Dezaki's *tome-e* (止め絵, held "stop" frames, often a painted still with a slow camera move) are used for dramatic and comic emphasis.
   - Thomas Lamarre (*The Anime Machine*, 2009) argues that limited animation is an aesthetic of its own. The feeling of movement comes from sliding layers ("multiplanar" composition) and camera moves over still images, not from drawing in-between frames.
   - Sources [O/E-low]:
     - BFI, "Looping around limitations: the experimental shorts of Tezuka Osamu": https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/features/looping-around-limitations-experimental-shorts-tezuka-osamu
     - Animation Obsessive, "When Osamu Tezuka Redefined Anime": https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/when-osamu-tezuka-redefined-anime
     - Rhizomes review of Lamarre: https://rhizomes.net/issue20/reviews/dunlap.html
     - Mechademia review: https://www.mechademia.net/2012/08/22/book-review-the-anime-machine/
     - Tsukuba frame-omission paper: https://www.cgg.cs.tsukuba.ac.jp/projects/2013/motion_frame_omission/data/IWAIT.pdf
4. **Camera moves on still images ("Ken Burns" pan and zoom).**
   - Editors and filmmakers widely say a pan or zoom points the viewer's eye "where you want it, when you want it."
   - **I found no controlled study showing that it raises retention.** Treat it as craft.
   - Sources [O]:
     - Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Burns_effect
     - Poynter, "Meaning in Motion": https://www.poynter.org/news/meaning-motion-ken-burns-and-his-effect
5. **Pose-to-pose with strong silhouettes.**
   - Classic animation manuals (Richard Williams, *The Animator's Survival Kit*; Preston Blair, *Cartoon Animation*; Disney's "staging" principle in Thomas & Johnston, *The Illusion of Life*) say a pose should read in silhouette: one clear idea per pose, with limbs held away from the body.
   - For stick figures the silhouette is nearly all of the acting.
   - Source [O, canonical craft; from general knowledge, not fetched this session].
6. **"Money shot": spend the motion budget on the punchline.** No study tests this. It is the logic behind the anime saving pattern above and Hanna-Barbera's "move only the part that matters." [O]
7. **Why audiences accept limited animation (inference):**
   - Viewers already read comics and storyboards, which are still images that tell a story.
   - Experiments on the Kuleshov effect (see §4) show viewers build meaning from how images are put in sequence, even when the images are still photographs.
   - Strong voice work and sound give the "life" that motion would otherwise supply. [Inference from E-med + O]

## 2. Comedy timing and joke structure

### Key findings

1. **Incongruity-resolution (Suls 1972).**
   - Step 1: the listener predicts an ending and the punchline breaks the prediction.
   - Step 2: the listener finds a rule that makes the punchline make sense.
   - Humour comes when the break is resolved; without resolution the listener is just puzzled.
   - Attardo extended it to setup, incongruity and resolution.
   - Hurley, Dennett & Adams (*Inside Jokes*, MIT Press 2011) say mirth is the brain's reward for catching a false assumption it had silently made.
   - **For writing:** the setup has to plant one wrong assumption on purpose, and the punchline has to expose it.
   - Sources [E-med theory, widely cited]:
     - Overview (Frontiers 2017): https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01835/full
     - Offscreen, "Humour: a synthesis": https://offscreen.com/view/humour-a-synthesis-pt2
     - *Inside Jokes* reviews: https://mindblog.dericbownds.net/2011/06/why-we-laugh.html
2. **Benign violation theory (McGraw & Warren 2010, *Psychological Science* 21(8)).**
   - Amusement comes when something is wrong (a violation) and okay at the same time (benign).
   - Three things make a violation benign: a second norm that says it's acceptable, weak commitment to the broken norm, and psychological distance.
   - McGraw et al. 2012 ("Too close for comfort, or too far to care?"): **tragedies are funnier when distant, small mishaps are funnier when close.**
   - **For writing:** everyday mishaps (late rent, a phone at 1%, a stubbed toe) should feel close and personal. Darker material such as a ghost's death needs distance: absurd framing and no real victim.
   - Sources [E-high, multiple experiments]:
     - https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf
     - https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.williams.leonard.2012.pdf
3. **Timing and the pause: the evidence is mixed.**
   - Pickering et al. 2009 and Attardo & Pickering 2011 measured recorded joke tellings acoustically.
     - Punchlines were **not** louder and were **not** reliably preceded by pauses.
     - In 2009 they were delivered at **lower pitch and slower rate**; Attardo & Pickering (2011) found no reliable change in speech rate.
   - A 2026 CogSci paper on 828 televised Chinese stand-up sets ("Timing is Everything") found **pauses lengthen before high-surprise punchlines.**
     - It reports that this pairing separates successful from unsuccessful sets, and that timing features predicted audience appreciation better than how incongruous the joke was.
     - It is newer and less reviewed, and TV editing may have affected the timing.
   - Reading of both: the pause is not universal. It may help when the punchline is a big surprise.
   - **For us:**
     - Deliver the punchline lower and slower, not louder.
     - Use a held beat only before the big reveal, not before every line.
   - Sources [E-med]:
     - https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/prosodic-markers-of-saliency-in-humorous-narratives/
     - https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/HUMR.2011.015/pdf?lang=en
     - [E-low, 2026] https://yzhu.io/publication/humor2026cogsci/
4. **Timing of comic sound and music.**
   - Kim, Kubo & Iwamiya (2016) found the best moment to add a "symbolic" comic music sting is **0.0-0.5 s after** a purely comic moment, and **0.5-1.0 s after** a tragicomic accident, where a short pause first works better.
   - Source [E-med] (via summary): https://labs.sonicfield.org/library/optimum-insertion-timing-of-symbolic-music-to-induce-laughter-in-video-contents
5. **Rule of three.**
   - No controlled study compares two, three and four beats. It is craft tradition: two items set a pattern and the third breaks it.
   - Psychologist Jyotsna Vaid supports it in an informal comment.
   - Sitcom writer Ken Levine says two items are often enough.
   - **For 30 s Shorts, two beats then the break is probably the right length.**
   - Sources [O]:
     - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_three_(writing)
     - https://tvwriter.com/ken-levine-gives-us-a-comedy-tip/
6. **Callbacks.**
   - Yuji Winet (Duke Fuqua): callback ads were remembered better two weeks later than ads shown twice, and callback jokes were enjoyed more than non-callback jokes.
   - Winet's analysis of about 5,500 jokes from Netflix specials found callbacks got longer laughs. That analysis is unpublished.
   - Source [E-med/E-low]: https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/how-marketers-can-harness-power-of-callbacks
7. **Processing fluency (Topolinski 2014, *Cognition & Emotion*).**
   - Showing a punchline word 1-15 minutes **before** the joke made the joke funnier; showing it **immediately before** made it less funny.
   - Gorenz & Schwarz (2026, unverified) also report that comedy is funnier when it is easier to process.
   - **For writing:** plant the punchline's key object early (seen, not mentioned), and keep wording simple so the "click" is instant for non-native speakers.
   - Sources [E-med]:
     - https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/spoilers-can-make-a-joke-funnier
     - https://www.psypost.org/comedy-is-funnier-when-it-is-easier-to-process-new-psychology-study-finds/
8. **Specific and funny-sounding words.**
   - Engelthaler & Hills 2018 (*Behavior Research Methods*) collected humour ratings for 4,997 words. Concrete, rude-ish and bodily words rate highest ("booty", "giggle", "goatee").
   - Westbury et al. 2016 (*J. Memory & Language*): made-up words with lower letter entropy (more unusual letter patterns, e.g. "snunkoople") are funnier, predictably so.
   - **For writing:** use concrete nouns, not abstract ones ("a single damp crouton" beats "some food").
   - Sources [E-high]:
     - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5990549
     - https://www.ualberta.ca/en/science/news/2015/november/the-snunkoople-effect.html
9. **Comic anticipation.**
   - Berk et al. (2006, conference data, n=16): just *expecting* a funny video raised beta-endorphin by 27%.
   - This is weak evidence. It fits the craft idea that a promise of a payoff is rewarding in itself.
   - Source [E-low]: https://phys.org/news63293074.html
10. **Laughter as social proof.**
    - Cai, Chen, White & Scott 2019 (*Current Biology*): adding laughter made weak jokes about 10% funnier (posed laughter) and 15-20% funnier (real laughter).
    - Baranowski et al. 2017: laugh tracks independently raised amusement in film clips.
    - We won't use a laugh track. A character's on-screen reaction (a snort from Snuggles) may do part of the same job; that is an inference.
    - Sources [E-med]:
      - https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/ha-ha-study-shows-laugh-tracks-make-dad-jokes-funnier-180972718/
      - https://experimental.psychologie.uni-mainz.de/files/2024/06/2017Baranowski-canned-laughter.pdf

## 3. Attention and retention in short-form video

### Key findings

1. **YouTube's own definitions (official).**
   - "Viewed vs. swiped away", now labelled **"Stayed to watch"**, is the share of times viewers stayed past the first few seconds of a Short shown in the feed.
   - The Shorts feed has no thumbnails or click-through, so YouTube's head of Shorts describes the first views as working like thumbnail impressions on long-form.
   - Since 31 Mar 2025 a Shorts view counts on start or replay. "Engaged views" keeps the older quality-filtered measure.
   - Average percentage viewed can go above 100% when viewers loop.
   - There is **no official benchmark.** Third-party targets (75-80% stayed, 80%+ average viewed) are vendor opinions.
   - Sources [official]:
     - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217?hl=en-GB&co=YOUTUBE._YTVideoType%3Dshorts
     - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281?hl=en&co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop
     - https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/renes-top-five-march-4-2024/
   - [E-low] https://contentstudio.io/blog/youtube-shorts-analytics-guide
2. **When people swipe (academic).**
   - Dashlet (NSDI 2023, Princeton) ran user studies on a TikTok-like app: **29% of swipes came in the first 20% of a video and 42% in the last 20%.**
   - Swipes cluster at the start (the hook failed) and near the end (the viewer predicts the payoff and leaves).
   - **This suggests our 0:18 cliff (45% of 40 s) is a mid-video sag.** The viewer has probably guessed where the joke is going, or nothing new has happened for several seconds.
   - Separate work (Augsburg QoE study) found that startup delay triples early swipes.
   - Sources [E-med]:
     - https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~ravian/publications/dashlet_nsdi23.pdf
     - https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/125900
3. **Hooks in the first 1-3 s.**
   - TikTok's creative best practices (quoted secondhand) say to state the core proposition in the first 3 s.
   - One vendor dataset (SMMNut) reports that starting the hook in second 1 raised 3-second retention from 63% to 81%.
   - Another vendor reports up to 50% better 3-second retention when the opening has text plus motion versus a static opening.
   - No method is published for either. The direction is consistent; the numbers are not trustworthy.
   - Source [E-low]: https://nestscale.com/?p=47200
4. **Curiosity gap.**
   - Loewenstein 1994 (*Psychological Bulletin*): curiosity is a felt deprivation caused by a gap in knowledge. It needs some prior knowledge and a gap that feels closable.
   - Kang et al. 2009 (*Psychological Science* 20(8)): curiosity activated reward regions (caudate). Curious people paid tokens or waited longer to see answers, and remembered surprising answers better.
   - **For writing:** open a specific question the viewer can almost answer. "Why is Penny charging rent for the fridge light?" is a closable gap. A vague mystery is not.
   - Sources [E-high]:
     - https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/
     - https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/the-wick-in-the-candle-of-learning-epistemic-curiosity-activates-/
5. **Cuts and visual change.**
   - Cutting, DeLong & Nothelfer 2010 (*Psychological Science*): average shot length in Hollywood fell from about 8-10 s (1960s) to 3-4 s (2005).
   - Shot lengths drifted toward a 1/f rhythm, varied rather than metronomic, which they link to natural swings in attention. Cutting stresses that this rhythm is not the same thing as being short.
   - Lang et al. 1993 (*Communication Research* 20(1)) built the Limited Capacity Model of mediated message processing. Cuts trigger an orienting response, an automatic shift of attention.
   - A secondhand report says more edits improved recognition memory up to about 10 cuts per 2 minutes, then it fell. This is unverified, and that rate is far slower than modern Shorts.
   - Basic attention research: the *onset* of motion grabs attention (Abrams & Christ 2003; from general knowledge).
   - **For us:** a hard cut or new pose every 2-4 s, with irregular spacing, should do some of the work that continuous motion would. That is an inference.
   - Sources [E-high/E-med]:
     - https://news.cornell.edu/node/271563
     - https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=30546
     - https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Geiger-HumanCommRes93.pdf
6. **Pattern interrupts every 2-4 s** (zoom, cut, sound effect) is vendor and creator advice [O/E-low]. It agrees with the cut research but has not been tested on Shorts specifically.
7. **Loops.**
   - Creators report that an ending which flows back into the opening raises rewatches, so average percentage viewed can go above 100%.
   - YouTube does not confirm a ranking effect. [O]

## 4. "Radio with pictures": audio carries the comedy

### Key findings

1. **Audio is the strongest cue for funny moments in video.**
   - FunnyNet-W (2024, a machine-learning paper) trained models to detect funny moments in real-world video. Audio was the *dominant* cue; video added complementary information.
   - Audio beat subtitles in scenes with strong acting or sound but little dialogue.
   - This is not human perception data, but it supports Chuck Jones's "illustrated radio" description.
   - Source [E-med]: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.04210
2. **Prosody.**
   - Punchlines tend to be delivered at lower pitch (Pickering 2009).
   - Irony and satire studies test low pitch and slow rate as irony cues: *Canadian J. Exp. Psych.* 2021, abstract only; https://www.dare.uva.nl/id/4f73c750-c2be-4dc6-bc5a-80745f60fb1a?page=2655
   - A 2026 text-to-speech preprint found **loudness**, not rate or pitch contour, drove how sarcastic speech sounded: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.09717
   - The evidence is thin and mixed [E-low/E-med]. For ElevenLabs: deadpan means low and flat; sarcasm reads through a pointed, slightly louder stress on one word.
3. **Laughter and sound effects raise amusement** (§2.10). Comic music placed 0-0.5 s after the gag works best (§2.4).
4. **Kuleshov effect: editing creates emotion.**
   - Mobbs et al. 2006 (fMRI, n=14): the same neutral face was rated as more emotional after an emotional context shot.
   - Barratt et al. 2016 (*Perception* 45(8), n=36) concluded "some sort of Kuleshov effect does in fact exist." Viewers matched neutral faces to the context emotion more than chance, though eye-tracking did not differ.
   - A 2019 PLOS ONE study **replicated it with still photographs**, which is directly relevant to a held-pose format.
   - The effect is weaker for first-time film viewers (Ildirar & Ewing 2018) and may depend on point-of-view editing that ties the look to the object.
   - **For us:** a neutral deadpan face held after a cut to an absurd object reads as a reaction. We do not need to draw expressions; the edit supplies them.
   - Sources [E-med]:
     - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5632723
     - https://research.cbs.dk/en/publications/does-the-kuleshov-effect-really-exist-revisiting-a-classic-film-e-2/
     - https://www.prolekare.cz/casopisy/plos-one/2019-10-3/an-examination-of-the-kuleshov-effect-using-still-photographs-116517?hl=en
     - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuleshov_effect

## 5. Evidence vs opinion at a glance

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Benign violation; distance makes tragedy funny and closeness makes mishaps funny | Evidence (multiple experiments) |
| Curiosity gap drives seeking and memory | Evidence |
| Kuleshov-style context changes how a neutral face reads, including with stills | Evidence, moderate effect |
| Swipes cluster at the start and end of short videos | Evidence (one system study) |
| Laughter and sound raise rated funniness | Evidence (moderate, some failed replications) |
| Punchlines are lower in pitch, not louder | Evidence (small samples) |
| Pause before the punchline | Mixed evidence |
| Concrete or odd-sounding words are funnier | Evidence |
| Callbacks get bigger laughs and are remembered better | Some evidence |
| Rule of three | Opinion and craft |
| Ken Burns move or zoom keeps attention on stills | Opinion and craft |
| "Hook in 1-3 s", "interrupt every 2-4 s" | Platform guidance and vendor data |
| Animate only the punchline ("money shot") | Opinion and craft (anime and Hanna-Barbera practice) |

## 6. Rules for a 25-35 s near-static stick-figure comedy short

Each rule gives the rule, the evidence behind it, and an example for the cast.

**R1. Write it as radio first.**
- **Rule:** The script must be funny as audio alone. Test it by listening to the voice track with the screen off.
- **Evidence:** Chuck Jones's "illustrated radio" (O); FunnyNet-W found audio is the dominant humour cue (E-med); Hanna-Barbera put its "life" in sound.
- **Example:** Snuggles, flat: "Steve has checked the oven is off. Four times. The oven is not plugged in." If that line doesn't land as audio, no pose will save it.

**R2. Open with the conflict or the strangest image in second 0-1. No setup.**
- **Rule:** Frame 1 is the problem already happening, with an on-screen caption.
- **Evidence:** YouTube's "Stayed to watch" metric measures the first seconds (official); swipes cluster in the first 20% (Dashlet, E-med); TikTok guidance on the first 3 s (E-low).
- **Example:** Frame 1 shows Penny holding a clipboard in front of Steve's toilet. Caption: "RENT IS DUE. ON THE TOILET."

**R3. Open a specific, closable question.**
- **Rule:** The hook must raise a question the viewer is sure will be answered within 30 s.
- **Evidence:** Loewenstein's information-gap theory; Kang 2009 (E-high).
- **Example:** Ted: "I've been haunting this flat for 40 years and nobody's noticed. Today I find out why." The viewer wants the "why."

**R4. Plant exactly one false assumption, then expose it.**
- **Rule:** Each joke has one setup belief and one reveal that makes sense once you see it.
- **Evidence:** Suls's incongruity-resolution model; Hurley-Dennett-Adams (E-med theory).
- **Example:** Nana: "I've been doing push-ups every morning." (We assume she's exercising.) Cut to Nana doing push-ups on top of a burglar.

**R5. Keep everyday mishaps close; give dark material distance.**
- **Rule:** Steve's problems should be ones everyone has had. Death, injury and money stress need absurd distance.
- **Evidence:** McGraw et al. 2012, close mishaps vs distant tragedies (E-high).
- **Example:** Steve's phone at 1% with the map open is close and relatable. Ted's death is told as bureaucracy ("Cause of death: replied-all"), never as suffering.

**R6. Change the frame every 2-4 s, and vary the spacing.**
- **Rule:** Something new appears every 2-4 s: a cut, a new held pose, a zoom, a prop. Do not use a fixed metronome.
- **Evidence:** Cuts trigger orienting responses (Lang 1993, E-med); 1/f varied shot rhythms (Cutting 2010, E-high); motion onset grabs attention (E-high); creator interrupt advice (O).
- **Example:**
  - 0-2 s: Steve sweating.
  - 2-5 s: close-up of the email subject.
  - 5-6 s: Snuggles.
  - 6-9 s: wide shot of Steve hiding under the desk.

**R7. Fix the middle (the 0:18 sag): add a turn at 40-60% of the runtime.**
- **Rule:** At about 12-18 s the situation must escalate or reverse with new information. Do not repeat the premise.
- **Evidence:** Swipes cluster when the viewer can predict the rest (Dashlet, E-med); a curiosity gap closes once it is guessable (Loewenstein).
- **Example:** Penny's rent rises with each line, then she reveals she bought the building next door. "You're also my neighbour now. Different rent."

**R8. Two beats, then the break (a compressed rule of three).**
- **Rule:** Use two examples to set the pattern and break it on the third. In Shorts, never use four.
- **Evidence:** Craft (O); Ken Levine says two is often enough.
- **Example:** Snuggles: "Steve's alarms: 6:00. 6:05. 'Steve, the building is on fire.'"

**R9. Give motion only to the punchline (the money shot).**
- **Rule:** Every other beat is a held pose. The punchline gets the one real movement: a snap pose change, a smash zoom, a prop flying, a smear frame.
- **Evidence:** Anime saves on low-motion shots for one key cut; Hanna-Barbera animated only the part that mattered (O); motion onset captures attention (E-high). A rare movement stands out more than constant movement.
- **Example:** Twenty seconds of Nana knitting, perfectly still. On the punchline, a single pop pose of her suplexing the vacuum cleaner.

**R10. Let the cut make the face (Kuleshov).**
- **Rule:** Hold a neutral or deadpan face, then cut to the absurd thing, then back to the same face. Do not draw "shocked" expressions.
- **Evidence:** Mobbs 2006; Barratt 2016; still-photo replication 2019 (E-med).
- **Example:** Cut from Snuggles's blank stare, to Steve wearing three hoodies in July, back to the same stare. The viewer supplies the judgement.

**R11. Silhouette-first poses, one idea per pose.**
- **Rule:** Each held pose must read as a black silhouette at phone size: arms away from the body, a clear line of action, a prop sticking out.
- **Evidence:** Disney staging, Williams, Blair (O, canonical).
- **Example:** Steve panicking is both arms up and knees bent, not arms crossed against his torso. Penny's clipboard should poke out past her head.

**R12. Deliver punchlines lower and slower, with a beat only before the big one.**
- **Rule:** In ElevenLabs direction, the punchline is lower in pitch and a little slower, never shouted. Put a held beat of about 0.4-0.8 s only before the biggest surprise.
- **Evidence:** Pickering 2009, lower and slower (E-med); 2026 CogSci, pauses before high-surprise punchlines (E-low).
- **Example:** Ted: "I'm not here to scare you... (beat) ...I'm here about the Wi-Fi password."

**R13. Put a sting or reaction sound 0-0.5 s after the gag.**
- **Rule:** The comic sound effect or musical sting lands immediately after the punchline, not on it and not a second later. For a pratfall or accident, wait about 0.5-1 s.
- **Evidence:** Kim, Kubo & Iwamiya 2016 (E-med); laughter raises funniness (Cai 2019, E-med).
- **Example:** Steve's chair collapses, then a 0.7 s gap, then a sad trombone. Or Snuggles's single "hm" right after Penny's line, acting as social proof.

**R14. Specific, concrete, international words.**
- **Rule:** Use concrete nouns and avoid idioms and wordplay that need native English. Short words carry better in captions.
- **Evidence:** Word-humour norms (Engelthaler & Hills 2018, E-high); fluency makes jokes funnier (Topolinski 2014, E-med).
- **Example:** Not "Steve's in a pickle," but "Steve is hiding from a parcel."

**R15. Plant early and call back, ideally into a loop.**
- **Rule:** Show the punchline's key object in the first 3 s without comment, and pay it off at the end. Make the last frame cut cleanly into the first.
- **Evidence:** Callback memory and enjoyment (Winet, E-med/E-low); Topolinski fluency, pre-exposure helps but not right before the punchline (E-med); loops can push average viewed past 100% (official metric note; benefit is opinion).
- **Example:**
  - 0:01: Nana's teacup is visible on the shelf.
  - 0:28: the burglar is revealed to be hiding behind that same teacup.
